How this works

The calculator simulates your quote desk one business day at a time for a full quarter (13 weeks), then repeats that quarter 200 times with different random luck. All the figures you see are summaries of those 200 quarters. The same inputs always give the same answer, so a shared link shows everyone the same result.

What it models

  • Two steps, in order. Every new RFQ is first logged and triaged by intake staff. Then it waits for the next free quoter. Each step works first-come, first-served.
  • The clock starts when the RFQ arrives and stops when the quote goes out. That includes time spent waiting for intake, which is often where days disappear without anyone noticing.
  • RFQs don’t arrive evenly. Your weekly average is the long-run rate, but some days bring a cluster and some bring none. That randomness is what builds queues even when the team looks “busy but OK” on paper.
  • Effort varies. Each RFQ takes a random amount of time around its class average. A few take much longer than average, which is normal for engineered work.
  • A workday is your productive hours, not 8. Each person does the number of productive hours you enter per day. Unfinished work carries over to the next business day. Turnaround is counted in business days (Monday to Friday).
  • Revisions are real work. The share of quotes you enter comes back for a re-quote 1–5 business days after the quote is sent. Revisions skip intake, join the back of the quoting queue, take the percentage of the original effort you enter, and count as their own quotes against the target.
  • RFQs already open start the quarter at the front of the quoting queue, already triaged. They’re assumed to have arrived over the last few days at your normal rate, so some are already partly through their target window.
  • Overload is never hidden. Every quote that arrives during the quarter is tracked until it’s actually finished, even if that happens after week 13. A quote still open at the end of the quarter and already past target counts as late.

How to read the results

  • Utilization is the work coming in divided by the hours available at that step. The step with the higher number is flagged as the bottleneck: adding people anywhere else won’t help much. Above about 85%, waits grow quickly, and one person running at 90% has almost no slack for a busy Monday. At 100% or more, the backlog grows every week and never recovers.
  • On time is the share of quotes, including revisions, finished within your target.
  • 90th percentile is the turnaround that 9 out of 10 quotes beat. It’s usually what customers remember.
  • Why an intake hire can make things look worse. When both steps are overloaded, adding intake can lower the on-time rate. Revisions and already-open RFQs skip intake, so while intake is the jam they slip past it. Clear intake and the queue moves to quoting, where they have to wait like everything else. New RFQs still get faster. The lesson: fix the step that will be the bottleneck after the hire, not just today’s.
  • Staff needed is the smallest team found that hits your on-time goal, adding one person at a time wherever it helps most. It can be smaller than your team today.

What it doesn’t know (limitations)

  • One shared quoter pool. It assumes any quoter can quote any RFQ. If engineered RFQs can only go to two specialists, your real bottleneck may be that sub-team, and the results here will look too optimistic. Dedicated sub-teams aren’t modeled in this version.
  • No priorities or expediting. Everything is first-come, first-served. Pulling hot RFQs to the front shortens their wait but lengthens everyone else’s.
  • Steady demand and staffing. No seasonality, trade-show spikes, vacations, sick days, holidays or ramp-up time for new hires. A new hire in this tool is fully productive on day one.
  • One person per quote at each step. No waiting on suppliers, engineering sign-off or customer clarifications. Those delays add calendar time on top of what’s shown here.
  • Your inputs drive everything. If the hours per RFQ are optimistic, so are the results. Treat the output as a direction and a sense of scale, not a forecast.

Verification

The simulation engine is checked against the textbook formula for waiting lines (Erlang C) in cases where an exact answer exists. It’s also checked for common-sense behavior: no RFQs means no backlog, and overload means a growing backlog. Adding a quoter never makes results worse. Adding intake never slows down new RFQs, but when quoting is overloaded it can lower the overall on-time rate, for the reason explained above.
